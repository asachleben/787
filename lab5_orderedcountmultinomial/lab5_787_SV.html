Overview

So far, we have focused on binary outcome variables. Today, we will extend that framework to three additional kinds of outcomes: count outcomes, ordered categorial outcomes, and multinomial outcomes.

Across each of these model types, we’ll estimate the appropriate model and see what information can be gathered from it. Then, we’ll generate and visualize (i.e., plot) predicted outcomes or predicted probabilities related to our predictors of interest. Note: the process we’ll focus on today is something that you’ll come back to time and time again in your own research, especially when thinking about how to best convey your results in a paper.

By the end of the lab, you should be able to fit and interpret Poisson/negative binomial, ordered logit, and multinomial logit models and generate substantively interpretable predictions from each.

Count Outcomes

We will begin with a count outcome. Count variables are discrete, bounded below at zero, and can only take non-negative integer values. Examples include the number of protests in a country-year, the number of bills sponsored by a legislator, or the number of executive orders issued by a governor.

For this section, we will use replication data from Barber, Bolton, and Thrower (2019) on executive unilateralism under divided government. Our dependent variable, num_eos, is the number of executive orders issued by a governor in a given month.

We will use five predictors:

  • divided_gov1: whether the governor serves under divided (1) or unified (0) government
  • veto_proof: whether there is a veto-proof majority in the state legislature
  • house_polarization: polarization in the lower chamber of the state legislature
  • governor_party_pres_vote: presidential vote share for the governor’s party
  • governor_power: an index of gubernatorial power

Poisson Regression

A standard starting point for count outcomes is the Poisson regression model. The Poisson model uses a log link, so the conditional mean of the outcome is constrained to be positive:

\[ \log(\lambda_i) = X_i\beta, \]

or equivalently,

\[ \lambda_i = \exp(X_i\beta), \]

where \(\lambda_i = E(Y_i\mid X_i)\).

A key assumption of the Poisson model is equidispersion:

\[ E(Y_i\mid X_i) = Var(Y_i\mid X_i). \]

In many political science applications, the variance is substantially larger than the mean. This is called overdispersion.

We can formally evaluate overdispersion using dispersiontest() from the AER package.

# test for overdispersion
__________(__________)

The null hypothesis is that the dispersion parameter equals 1, as assumed by the Poisson model. Here, the estimated dispersion parameter is approximately 83.6, meaning that the variance is substantially larger than the mean. The small p-value (\(p < 0.001\)) provides strong evidence of overdispersion, suggesting that the Poisson model is not appropriate for these data. In this case, it therefore likely makes sense to pivot to a negative binomial model.

Negative Binomial Regression

The negative binomial model relaxes the Poisson assumption that the conditional mean and variance are equal. It is therefore often useful when the data are overdispersed.

Ordered Outcomes

An ordered dependent variable contains categories with a meaningful ranking, but we cannot assume that the distance between adjacent categories is constant.

For example, consider agreement with the statement:

“A working mother can establish just as warm and secure of a relationship with her child as a mother who does not work.”

Respondents can answer:

  1. Strongly disagree
  2. Disagree
  3. Agree
  4. Strongly agree

These categories have a clear ordering, but the difference between “Strongly disagree” and “Disagree” is not necessarily the same as the difference between “Agree” and “Strongly agree.”

We will use data from the 1977 and 1989 General Social Survey. The predictors are:

  • male: respondent is male
  • white: respondent is white
  • age: respondent age
  • ed: years of education
  • prst: occupational prestige

Read the data and prepare it for analysis.

# load data
hw <- read_csv(
  url("http://faculty.washington.edu/cadolph/mle/ordwarm2.csv"),
  show_col_types = FALSE
)

# reshape
hw <- hw %>%
  mutate(
    warm = case_when(
      warm == 1 ~ "Strongly disagree",
      warm == 2 ~ "Disagree",
      warm == 3 ~ "Agree",
      warm == 4 ~ "Strongly agree"
    ),
    warm = factor(
      warm,
      levels = c(
        "Strongly disagree",
        "Disagree",
        "Agree",
        "Strongly agree"
      ),
      ordered = TRUE
    )
  )

Multinomial Outcomes

Lastly, we’ll look at unordered categorical dependent variable with more than two categories, or a multinomial outcome. In this case, the values represent different alternatives but there is no meaningful ordering among them.

We will use data from the 2017/18 round of LAPOP in Brazil and examine reported vote choice among three candidates in the 2018 presidential election: Jair Bolsonaro, Fernando Haddad, and Ciro Gomes. There is no defensible ordering such as Bolsonaro < Haddad < Gomes, so an ordered model would not be appropriate. The predictors are:

  • sex: male (1) or female (0)
  • age: respondent age
  • capital_pun: support for capital punishment (1) or not (0)

Load the BrazilLAPOP_2018.rds data and clean it to fit a model.

# load data
brazil_data <- read_rds("BrazilLAPOP_2018.rds")

# filter data
brazil_data <- subset(brazil_data, 
                      vote_choice == "1501" | 
                        vote_choice == "1502" |
                        vote_choice == "1503")
brazil_data$vote_choice <- as.factor(brazil_data$vote_choice)

# rename levels to candidate names
brazil_data$vote_choice <- factor(
  brazil_data$vote_choice,
  levels = c("1501", "1502", "1503"),
  labels = c("Bolsonaro", "Haddad", "Gomes")
)

The first factor level is the reference category. With the ordering above, the model will estimate separate equations comparing Haddad with Bolsonaro and Gomes with Bolsonaro.
