Model Evaluation and Interpretation

Last week, we focused on estimating models for binary dependent variables. We used glm() to estimate logit and probit models and discussed why the raw coefficients from these models are not directly interpretable as changes in probability.

Today, we’re going to shift from estimating a model to thinking about what we actually do with that model once we have it. We’ll focus on two related tasks.

First, we’ll work on model interpretation. We’ll use simulation to calculate predicted probabilities and incorporate uncertainty in our coefficient estimates. In doing so, we’ll compare two approaches to generating predictions: the average-case approach and the observed-case approach.

Second, we’ll turn to model evaluation. We’ll use the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under that curve (AUROC) to evaluate how well our model distinguishes between observations for which the outcome occurred and observations for which it did not.

Throughout the lab, we’ll try to explain the following question: what predicts whether congressional candidates discuss immigration on their campaign websites?

Simulating Predicted Probabilities

Our fitted model gives us a vector of coefficient estimates, \(\hat{\beta}\), and a variance-covariance matrix describing the uncertainty in those estimates.

Rather than treating the estimated coefficients as known with certainty, we can draw many sets of coefficients from their approximate sampling distribution. For each simulated set of coefficients, we then calculate the predicted probability we care about.

First, set a seed for reproducibility and choose the number of simulations. We’ll use 1,000.

# set seed for reproducibility
set.seed(9692)

# number of simulations

We’ll use mvrnorm() from the MASS package to draw 1,000 simulated coefficient vectors from a multivariate normal distribution centered on our estimated coefficients. The function takes three main arguments:

  • n specifies the number of simulated coefficient vectors we want to draw

  • mu specifies the mean of the multivariate normal distribution. We set this equal to our estimated coefficients from the logit model.

  • Sigma specifies the variance-covariance matrix of the multivariate normal distribution. We use vcov(model_logit), which contains both the estimated variance of each coefficient and the covariance between coefficients.

Using the full variance-covariance matrix is important because the uncertainty in our coefficient estimates is not necessarily independent. mvrnorm() preserves these relationships when drawing sets of coefficients.

Each row of beta_sims is one simulated set of coefficients, while each column corresponds to one coefficient in our fitted model.

Because we drew these simulated coefficients from a multivariate normal distribution centered on the coefficient estimates from model_logit, the average value of each simulated coefficient should be very close to the corresponding coefficient from the original model. However, the simulated means will generally not be exactly identical to the original estimates because we are drawing a finite number of random samples from that distribution. If we increased the number of simulations substantially, the simulated means would generally move even closer to the estimated coefficients.

The key idea is that we can now calculate our quantity of interest under 1,000 simulated versions of our fitted model. The distribution of those simulated quantities gives us a way to summarize both our point estimate and our uncertainty.

We’ll use these same coefficient simulations with two different approaches to generating predicted probabilities.

Average-Value Approach

One traditional way to interpret a nonlinear model is to construct a hypothetical observation. The basic idea is to:

  1. Pick the predictor we care about.
  2. Allow it to vary over a meaningful range.
  3. Hold the other variables at selected values.
  4. Calculate the predicted probability at each value.

Our predictor of interest is district_lean. Let’s create a sequence covering its observed range in the estimation sample.

# create sequence over observed range of district lean
district_vec <- seq(
  from = min(lab_data$district_lean),
  to = max(lab_data$district_lean),
  length.out = 100
)

Now we need to decide who our hypothetical candidate is.

Importantly, the average-value approach does not mean that we should blindly take the mathematical mean of every variable. What would it mean to have a candidate who is 47% Republican or 0.31 incumbents? For categorical variables, we have to select an actual category.

For this example, we’ll examine an inexperienced Democratic male challenger facing a contested primary. We’ll hold fundraising at its sample mean and vary only district_lean.

This second question points to an important limitation of the average-case approach. We get an intuitive prediction for one clearly defined hypothetical candidate, but the marginal change in predicted probability can depend on which hypothetical candidate we choose. For example, if we were comparing among Republican incumbent candidates, there might be a different marginal effect for district_lean on the probability of discussing immigration.

Observed-Case Approach

An alternative is the observed-case approach.

Instead of constructing one hypothetical candidate and holding the other predictors at selected values, we’ll retain the actual values of the other covariates for every candidate in our estimation sample.

For each value of district_lean, we’ll:

  1. Take every candidate in the estimation sample.
  2. Set district_lean equal to that value for everyone.
  3. Leave candidate type, fundraising, party, primary competition, and gender at their observed values.
  4. Calculate a predicted probability for every candidate.
  5. Average those predicted probabilities across candidates.
  6. Repeat this process for every simulated set of coefficients.

This gives us the average predicted probability across the candidates represented in our estimation sample at each hypothetical value of district_lean.

First, pull the original design matrix from our fitted model.

# create design matrix using observed covariate values
observed_matrix <- model.________(model_logit)

# inspect dimensions
dim(observed_matrix)

We’ll create an empty matrix to store our simulated average predicted probabilities. Each row will represent one coefficient simulation and each column will represent one value of district_lean.

# initialize matrix for observed-case simulation results
observed_sims <- matrix(
  NA,
  nrow = n_sim,
  ncol = length(district_vec)
)

Now loop through the different hypothetical values of district_lean.

# calculate observed-case predicted probabilities
for (j in seq_along(district_vec)) {

  # make temporary copy of observed design matrix
  temporary <- observed_matrix

  # replace district lean for ALL candidates
  # with current hypothetical value
  temporary[, "district_lean"] <- district_vec[j]

  # calculate predicted probabilities for every candidate
  # under every simulated set of coefficients
  pp <- plogis(
    temporary %*% t(beta_sims)
  )

  # average across candidates for each coefficient simulation
  observed_sims[, j] <- colMeans(pp)
}

There are several steps happening inside this loop, so let’s unpack them.

At each value of district_lean, every candidate is temporarily assigned the same hypothetical district lean. Their other characteristics remain exactly as observed. We then calculate a predicted probability for every candidate under every simulated coefficient vector and average those predictions across candidates.

Importantly, we take one additional average in the observed-case approach. Rather than calculating the predicted probability for one hypothetical candidate, we first calculate predictions for all of the observed candidates and then average over those predictions.

The two lines should follow a broadly similar substantive pattern, but they are not identical. This is not a coding error!

The average-case approach asks about one hypothetical candidate whose other characteristics we selected. The observed-case approach asks what the average predicted probability would be across the candidates represented in our estimation sample while changing only district_lean.

Because the logit model is nonlinear, calculating a prediction for a candidate with selected or “average” characteristics is not generally the same thing as calculating predictions for all of the observed candidates and then averaging those predictions.

Evaluating Model Discrimination

So far, we’ve focused on interpreting our model. We’ve asked how the predicted probability of discussing immigration changes as district_lean changes.

We can also ask a different question: how well does the model distinguish between candidates who actually discuss immigration and candidates who do not?

Our model gives every candidate a predicted probability between 0 and 1. We could choose a threshold—for example, 0.50—and classify candidates with predicted probabilities above that threshold as discussing immigration. But 0.50 is ultimately an arbitrary cutoff. A different threshold would produce a different combination of correctly and incorrectly classified observations.

The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve evaluates this tradeoff across all possible classification thresholds.

For each possible threshold, the ROC curve compares:

  • the true-positive rate, or the proportion of observed 1s correctly identified as 1s; and
  • the false-positive rate, or the proportion of observed 0s incorrectly identified as 1s.

A model that does a better job separating the two groups will produce an ROC curve that bows farther toward the upper-left corner of the plot. A model with no discriminatory ability will fall along the 45-degree diagonal.

ROC Curve

First, we’ll generate the ROC curve using the fitted probabilities from our model. The roc() function needs two main pieces of information:

  • predictions is a numeric vector containing the model’s predicted probability for each observation. Here, we use predict(model_logit, type = “response”), which returns the fitted probability that each candidate discusses immigration.
  • labels contains the actual observed outcome for each of those same observations. In our case, these are the observed values of immigration, coded 0 or 1.

Importantly, these two vectors line up observation by observation. For each candidate, predictions tells us what probability the model assigned to discussing immigration, while labels tells us whether that candidate actually discussed immigration.

Area Under the ROC Curve

Rather than interpreting the entire ROC curve visually, we can summarize its discriminatory ability using the area under the ROC curve (AUROC). You will also commonly see this referred to simply as the AUC.

AUROC ranges from 0 to 1. A value of 0.5 indicates no discriminatory ability, while a value of 1 indicates perfect discrimination. Values below 0.5 indicate that the model is systematically ranking the two groups in the wrong direction.

A useful substantive interpretation is to imagine randomly selecting one candidate who discusses immigration and one candidate who does not. The AUROC is the probability that the model assigns the candidate who discusses immigration the higher predicted probability.

Note: The ROC curve and AUROC above are calculated using the same observations used to estimate the model. They therefore describe the model’s in-sample discrimination. They do not tell us how well the model would perform on entirely new candidates that were not used to estimate it. Evaluating out-of-sample performance requires a separate test sample or a procedure such as cross-validation.

AUROC also evaluates discrimination, not whether the predicted probabilities are perfectly calibrated. A model can rank 1s above 0s relatively well without every predicted probability matching the corresponding observed frequency.
